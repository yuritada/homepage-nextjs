# Giken-haku 2026 Vol.2: Three and a Half Hours of Talking Campus DX, Two Corporate Prizes, and On to Giken-ten

On September 19, 2026, I exhibited solo at **Giken-haku 2026 Vol.2**, bringing **[MIRAIS](/blog/2026-07-14-mirais)**, my campus DX portal.

![The "GeekHaku" logo monument standing in the venue](/photos/2026-09-19-giikuhaku-2026/giikuhaku2026_logo.jpg)

Out of nearly 150 applying teams, 51 were selected to exhibit. Everything from hardware to web services was on display, and the venue had the energy of a mixed-martial-arts tournament between wildly different disciplines.

Under the judging rules — seven sponsor companies each picking up to three teams, overlaps allowed, so 21 slots at most — I came away with corporate prizes from **SHIFT Inc.** and **DeNA Co., Ltd.** Being recognised by two companies through such a narrow gate, and knowing the intent and the energy behind the project landed correctly, is honestly a very happy result.

But more than the prizes, the real takeaway was the resolution I gained on *how to explain things*, and finally putting my thinking about DX into words — earned by standing at that booth for three and a half straight hours. Here's the write-up, while it's all still warm.

## 1. A Room That Wanted to Listen, and Rapid PDCA on My Pitch

The first thing I felt walking in was the sheer intellectual curiosity and appetite for conversation — from the visiting companies, from the presenting students, from the organisers, everyone.

Whenever I called out to someone passing by — "would you like to take a look?" — they stopped and leaned in. Getting feedback that went deep enough to ask "what exactly would you improve here?" and "what concerns shaped this design?" made it an enormously rewarding place to be a developer.

Because I exhibited solo, every conversation was one-on-one, and over three and a half hours I ended up delivering the pitch more than a dozen times. That turned out to be the perfect laboratory.

- Which phrases made people nod
- Where a faint question mark appeared
- Which angle made the value land immediately

Collecting the audience's reactions as live "data" and tuning the structure of the pitch run by run — learning, in practice, how to get a product's value across to a person accurately — mattered as much to me as the prizes did.

## 2. DX Is Not One Big Flower — It's a Field You Grow Patiently

The framing that worked best as the spine of my presentation was "campus DX." Being able to place the project in a single phrase raised the resolution of every conversation that followed.

It also pushed me to think hard about what the "DX" I work on actually is.

At the typical hackathon or competition, what tends to get rewarded is the single big showy flower — a feature or a gimmick whose impressiveness is obvious at a glance.

DX that solves real problems in work and daily life doesn't work that way.

I don't believe there is any convenient "big flower" in DX that single-handedly transforms the whole experience.

You move your feet, you face the problems, you pack down the ground — and only when countless small sprouts, buds and delicate blossoms open gently on top of it does the whole become a beautiful field of flowers. That is what DX really is.

Which is exactly why this kind of project doesn't come across unless you explain the background carefully. Of the enormous number of features that got built, only the tip of the iceberg is visible. The small considerations and thoughtful adjustments that bring the experience closer to what users actually want are the first things to get buried under a flashy presentation.

And yet each of those unglamorous refinements reliably raises the finished quality of the product, and translates directly into delight for the people who use it. Talking it through with visitors reaffirmed how valuable it is to keep asking "how do we remove someone's frustration?" and "how do we make a messy process smooth?" rather than betting everything on a one-shot idea.

## 3. Connections, and What Other Booths Taught Me About How Work Gets Judged

Another thing that stayed with me is how strongly an offline event makes you feel the community around it.

Plenty of exhibitors were people I already knew, so the day doubled as a reunion — catching up, showing each other what we'd built. On top of that I connected with many well-known figures and developers in the scene for the first time, which drove home again how much it matters to actually show up and talk in person.

Walking the other booths was dense input too. Standing in front of other teams' sharp products — hardware especially — I could watch up close which angles made judges and company reps react, and where the real evaluation points sit. As an exhibitor, that was a serious lesson.

## 4. Next Stop: Giken-ten

Analysing what other booths were rewarded for, and validating my own pitch at speed at my booth — together, those gave me both confidence and practical experience about what to say, and how, at Giken-ten, one of Japan's largest competitions.

I'm going to keep sharpening this unglamorous but genuinely valuable challenge called "campus DX," and take it to the Giken-ten stage next.

To everyone who stopped by the booth, everyone who gave me valuable feedback, the fellow exhibitors who pushed me, and the organisers who built such a great venue — thank you so much!
