# Google Gemini Day: What a Geek Found in a Community That Bets Everything on Ideas

On September 25, 2026, I went to "Google Gemini Day," held on the 33rd floor of Shibuya Stream at Google's Shibuya office.

![The reception counter at Google's office, with the colourful Google logo mounted on a brick wall](/photos/2026-09-25-gemini-day/gemini_logo.jpg)

*▲ Reception at Google's Shibuya office, 33rd floor of Shibuya Stream*

The world I normally live in is the geek scene — picking architectures, writing deep code, arguing about technical debt and low-level details. What I walked into this time was a student community with a completely different flavour from that technology-first world. While silently wondering about the ceiling height ("wait, isn't this kind of low? Or are other offices just tall?"), I could feel that one evening of this energy would be enough to turn me into a full-time Gemini evangelist.

![Holding a Google guest pass reading "Group J, Yuri", with the café-style venue spreading out behind it](/photos/2026-09-25-gemini-day/gemini_nametag.jpg)

Here's everything I scribbled into my notes, the energy of the community as I felt it, and my own take from a geek's point of view.

## A Flood of Gemini Updates: Google's "Lock-In" and the Energy of a Student Community

The Gemini updates shared at the start all made one thing clear: Google is serious about supporting students and growing this community.

- **Student Hub & the return of the free tier**: Along with a student-facing notebook environment ("student Notebook"), Gemini's free tier has been strengthened again.
- **Freemium Competition**: A contest to spread awareness of the student plan and compete on new sign-ups. Winners get a shot at attending YTFF (YouTube FanFest). "So this is how you use the power of a student community to grow subscriptions and lock people in. Sneaky (lol)" — I couldn't help the internal commentary, but the drive they build by pulling students in is genuinely well done.
- **Gemini Live evolving**: Faster responses, of course, but also integration with existing chat apps and the ability to run Deep Research mid-voice-conversation — new value beyond mere speed.
- **Gemini Spark**: Stronger scheduling. It ties tightly into Gmail to gather information, upgrading the old notion of "tasks" into "skills → schedule."
- **File Gen Edit & AI Plus**: File editing that's reminiscent of Claude Cowork. AI Plus adds unlimited file uploads.
- **Beefed-up interactive visualisation**: Visualising data and talking to it have become even more seamless.
- **Spreading the culture**: Talk of "maybe Gemini's AI will show up at your university festival?" and a "Look at what you can do!" message made the intent clear — grow the Gemini ecosystem through student community power.

## Student Lightning Talks: Eight Presentations and the Power of "One Feature, One Breakthrough"

The lightning talks that followed weren't about technical difficulty at all — every one of them was fully committed to "how do I hack this everyday problem with Gemini?"

### 1. Auto-translating English sessions (Shizuoka University)

A practical example of translating English-language sessions in real time to knock down the language barrier.

### 2. No camera needed: making photos "feel something" with Gemini (Tohoku University of Art and Design)

A hack for converting photos into a retro look by feeding prompts into Gemini, no actual camera required. The trick to adding retro texture without altering the subject: specify it in concrete words. It was fascinating to see someone intuitively land on the most basic and most powerful approach in prompt engineering.

### 3. "KOKUDO," a new app from a medical app development committee (Tokyo University of Science)

The latest from a committee that has already won at the Tokyo Governor's Cup and Giken-haku. The concept: "run the national highways." Rather than showing distance travelled in plain kilometres, the app expresses it in terms of national highway routes — a genuinely unusual angle. An iOS/Android release is planned. Gemini's role covered idea generation and animation: they converted video generated with Omni into GIFs and built it into the app, properly folding generative AI into their graphics pipeline.

### 4. An app for tracking where lab members are (Saitama University)

A case of combining their own major — wireless communications — with system development. Packet sensors, a Raspberry Pi and Wireshark together capture and analyse packets to determine whether a member has entered or left the room.

The striking part is the pace: built in three days, released the next (fast…). They handed the parts outside their expertise, or that they simply disliked, to Gemini — having clearly defined "Gemini as the means to realise my own idea" and then used it to the hilt.

### 5. Making daily life more creative (Kochi University of Technology)

A talk from the "AI Video Research Group," apparently an internal organisation within the Gemini student community. Their account "kawaii.animal.bee" has hit two million views. They're chasing the question of how to produce exactly the images and videos you picture, and spoke of infinite possibility in "Omni Flash 1.1," which generates prompts automatically. Their focus on "beyond going viral" — sublimating this into a genuine specialisation in video production — really showed.

### 6. Tako: an on-premise internal knowledge base grounded in evidence and approval (Toyohashi University of Technology)

A presentation themed "turning scattered information into team knowledge anyone can verify." Against the problem of dispersed information, they ingest sources through MCP (Model Context Protocol), structure a knowledge base, build an appropriate RAG setup and feed it into the Gemini API.

My personal read is that the RAG construction itself is a fairly standard configuration — but a solid design combining an on-premise environment with MCP, built by students, is seriously well put together.

### 7. Lately I'm inseparable from Antigravity (Kanagawa University of Human Services, graduate school)

Working with video generation models like Google Flow (nano banana, omni and so on), you hit a wall: ten seconds is the limit for a generated clip. The talk was about using Antigravity to cut those ten-second clips together. It reportedly recognises the shot breakdown and even the tempo of the music and where the beats land. Their line about tools — "in the end, putting the idea into words is what matters most" — got a firm nod from me.

### 8. "Yuru-Gemi," a podcast you can just listen to (Ehime University / Yokohama City University)

A podcast programme called "Yuru-Gemi," built around "socialising without having to speak." It's a measure against a very real problem: the community has reached around 1,000 members, but the active rate is low — a lot of lurkers.

They run segments like "this week's Gemini moment" (turning analogue teaching materials into AI-generated tests, building a VR drawing game) and random talk themes, and store everything they accumulate in NotebookLM. They're recruiting guests and MCs, and the next lightning talk event is in December.

## "Gemini Challenge": All-In on Ideas

The event also introduced "Gemini Challenge," a build competition held twice a month.

There are five categories: creative and entertainment, upgrading school life, DX for clubs and organisations, new feature/app/tool development, and other.

Looking at the submissions, the tech stacks and the difficulty of the implementations are honestly not high. What stands out instead is the sharpness of the angle and the problem framing — "how do I deliver value to a user by breaking through on one single feature?" It feels like a Giken CAMP hackathon compressed to its absolute minimum.

Since you can't muscle your way through with technology, it's pure idea strength and observation that get tested. That's its own kind of difficulty — an "all-in on ideas" world with its own knacks and instincts. And precisely because the bar to entry is set low, it looks like a genuinely interesting place to take a swing.

## What Someone from the Geek Scene Felt on Touching "Route B"

Standing in that room and talking with presenters and attendees, one thing hit me hard.

There is, right here, another community of excellent people — one of a completely different type from the tech scene I normally inhabit.

![Attendees standing in a circle chatting on a floor with a "Gemini Day" panel behind them](/photos/2026-09-25-gemini-day/gemini_casual_talk.jpg)

*▲ The networking time after the talks. This was where the real takeaways came from*

The community I usually live in is where geeks who love deep technical conversation and want to push programming itself gather. Debates at the implementation layer — "how should we build this?", "what should the architecture be?" — are an everyday occurrence.

The products and projects the people at Gemini Day were working on were, in a sense, the exact opposite. In terms of technical and conceptual level, much of it was the kind of thing a first-year undergraduate, or someone at their first or second hackathon, starts with. (To put it with a bit of an edge: more than a few themes felt like "classic hackathon-beginner material.")

But here's what's genuinely remarkable. None of this was coming from CS students who write code every day — it was coming naturally from people with no technical background at all, humanities students who have never programmed, layers who had never touched development before. Seen that way, comparing them to hackathon-regular geeks is missing the point entirely.

Instead of forcing their way into the deep end of technology, they are **using Gemini and the services around it to their fullest, throwing an overwhelming number of attempts at overwhelming speed.**

The problems they tackle aren't lofty business plans or abstract pain points either — they're problems within a few metres of themselves: "this small everyday inconvenience," "who's in and out of my lab," "I want to make a photo feel something."

At a glance it might look technically shallow. But these people are seriously sharp, and above all their appetite for trying things and the sheer number of attempts they make is off the charts. Precisely because they aren't attached to technology, they use AI lightly and make things real. If any of them got serious about learning the technical side, they'd become "strong at both tech and ideas" in no time.

For someone on the technical side like me, simply witnessing which features of ChatGPT or Gemini catch an AI beginner's eye, and how they understand what AI even is, was a huge takeaway. Features and small use cases that I and the geeks around me would absolutely scroll past — they see possibility in them and turn them into products.

Hardening into nothing but the familiar "Route A" of pushing technology deeper narrows your field of view. Moving regularly between communities like this one — "Route B," hacking broad and shallow with flexible ideas and volume — and going back and forth across that difference in altitude and perspective is exactly what widens the range of your thinking as a builder.

I'm really glad I went. This is a community worth watching, and worth coming back to.
